## What the ruling does

Six weeks into a fight it has now agreed to hear, the Supreme Court told the Trump administration it may keep deporting people to countries that are not their own — countries never named in their removal orders — while the case is decided. The Court did it on the emergency docket, in an unsigned one-paragraph order, without explaining why. By a 6-3 vote it stayed a February judgment by Boston-based District Judge Brian Murphy that had barred the practice unless the government first told a person where they were being sent and gave them a real chance to say they would be tortured there. Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson noted that they would have denied the stay. Nothing about whether the policy is legal has been decided; the merits are set for argument in December.

## The 40-minute stay

Read this order for what it settles and the answer is almost nothing — but read it for how it was issued and the answer is unsettling. The government's reply brief landed, and the stay came down roughly forty minutes later. That pace tells you the majority had already made up its mind, and it fits a now-familiar rhythm on the emergency docket: grant the real-world relief first, defer the reasoning indefinitely. The trouble is that in a deportation case the relief *is* the whole ballgame. Once a person is flown to a third country, no December argument and no summer opinion can fly them back. A judgment that took Murphy an exhaustive record and the First Circuit an affirmance to build was set aside in the time it takes to read the reply.

## What this favors

It favors the executive's ability to act first and defend later — to move people across borders while the courts are still deciding whether it may. The stay standard, in the majority's silent hands, weighs the government's asserted harm from an injunction and not the irreversible harm to a noncitizen shipped somewhere they have never been and fear to go. It favors a maximal reading of removal authority under 8 U.S.C. §1231(b), and a narrow one of the notice and anti-torture protections that Congress and the Convention Against Torture built into the system. And it favors the emergency docket itself as a governing tool: a single unexplained order, with no one's name on it, reshaping who can be deported and how, months before the Court says what the law is.

## The aftermath

Removals to third countries can resume now, under the same guidance Murphy found unlawful, and some of them will be carried out before the Court ever reaches the merits. That is the point of a stay, and it is also its danger here: the people sent away in the interim get whatever process the guidance provides, not what the district court ordered. In December the justices will hear three questions — whether the district court had jurisdiction at all, whether it could vacate the guidance classwide under the APA, and whether the guidance is unlawful under the immigration statute, due process, and the anti-torture regime. A decision should come by the end of the term. By then the interim record — where people were sent, and what happened to them — will be part of the argument, whether the majority wanted it to be or not.

## My take

Taking the case is defensible; the questions are real, and the reach of a nationwide vacatur is exactly the kind of thing the Court should settle. Granting the stay this way is not. To lift a fully reasoned judgment about the risk of torture — without a word of explanation, forty minutes after the last brief, on a docket built for genuine emergencies — is to treat the irreversible as reversible. The majority may yet be right on the merits in June. But it has let the government do the one thing that cannot be undone before it has said whether the government is allowed to do it at all. The three dissenters did not need to write to be right about the stakes: some harms don't wait for a merits calendar.
