## What the ruling does

A Libertarian wanted the Supreme Court to put her back on the ballot in one of the country's tightest House races. Justice Samuel Alito, acting as Circuit Justice for the Third Circuit, said no — Friday night, in a single line, with no reasoning and without troubling the other eight. That is the whole order: an emergency application denied. Lana Leguía, who had qualified for New Jersey's 7th District only to be knocked off when a state appellate court reinstated a residency rule for petition circulators, is out. The two-term Republican incumbent, Tom Kean Jr., will now face Democrat Rebecca Bennett with no Libertarian name beside theirs — in a district that could be decided by a hair.

## The unanswered First Amendment question

Do not mistake this for a ruling that New Jersey's law is constitutional. The real question Leguía raised is a live one: whether a state may require the people who circulate a candidate's nominating petition to be registered voters within its borders. A federal court struck down New Jersey's identical rule for *primary* petitions in 2021, and the Secretary of State, relying on that decision, had put Leguía back on the ballot — before a state appellate panel reversed him, holding not that the rule was valid but that an executive officer cannot simply decline to enforce a statute the legislature never repealed. Alito's one-line denial leaves that constitutional question exactly where it was: unanswered, and now un-answerable before the ballots print.

## What this favors

The immediate beneficiary is the New Jersey Republican State Committee, whose lawyers built this challenge signature by signature, and the Republican incumbent it protects. Strip a Libertarian off the ballot in a razor-thin district and you remove a candidate who tends to pull from the right; in a race this close, that is worth real votes to Tom Kean Jr. It also favors the *Purcell* instinct that courts should not reshuffle an election already under way — vote-by-mail ballots were already going out. The loser is not only Leguía but the voters who wanted a third option, and the broader principle that ballot access should not turn on where a signature-gatherer happens to live.

## The aftermath

Leguía sits out 2026 as a printed candidate; a write-in campaign is all that is left to her this cycle. But the residency rule she attacked is still on the books, and the federal civil-rights suit that produced this emergency application is not over — the district court can still reach the merits on a full record, unhurried by the election calendar. Expect the circulator-residency question to come back on a slower track, where the *Buckley v. American Constitutional Law Foundation* line of cases — which treats petition circulation as core political speech — can actually be briefed and argued. This was a fire drill; the real fight is still pending.

## My take

On the clock, Alito's shrug is defensible: you cannot responsibly decide a serious First Amendment question in the days before ballots print, and *Purcell* counsels restraint once an election is under way. But be honest about the cost. New Jersey's own Secretary of State thought its residency rule unconstitutional, and a federal court had already said as much about the twin rule for primaries. A candidate who cleared the signature threshold three times over gets erased from the ballot on a technicality about where three volunteers lived — and the nation's highest court waves it through without a word. The tidy procedural answer is available. The uncomfortable substantive one — why a rule a federal court already condemned is still knocking third-party candidates off New Jersey ballots — is still sitting there, waiting for a court with the calendar to face it.
