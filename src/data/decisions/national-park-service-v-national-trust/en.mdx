## What the ruling does

The Supreme Court just handed President Trump a green light to keep pouring concrete for his East Wing ballroom — and it did it the way it now does almost everything consequential: on the emergency docket, unsigned, 5-4, without ruling on whether any of this is legal. The order stays a preliminary injunction that a federal district judge entered in April and that the D.C. Circuit had affirmed only weeks earlier. The majority's stated reason is that the National Trust for Historic Preservation probably cannot even be in court — that its interest in the historic White House grounds is not the kind of concrete injury Article III requires. Everything else, including whether Congress ever authorized bulldozing the East Wing, is left for another day. By then the ballroom may be finished.

## The Chief breaks ranks

The startling name in this case is not in the majority — it is in the dissent. Chief Justice John Roberts, who has spent much of the past two years finding ways to accommodate this administration, wrote six pages saying the project "is likely unlawful," joined by all three of the Court's liberals. He leaned on a statute that flatly forbids putting up any building on federal parkland or public grounds in Washington "without express authority of Congress," invoked the historic dignity of the place, and brushed aside the Government's national-security theory in a single dismissive footnote. When the Chief Justice is the one warning that the Court is letting an unauthorized structure go up on the nation's most symbolic ground, the 5-4 line is worth staring at.

## What this favors

This favors the executive's now-familiar strategy: build first, invoke national security, and let the courts sort out legality after the facts on the ground are irreversible. It favors a narrow, aggressive reading of standing that can quietly close the courthouse door to preservation and environmental plaintiffs whose injuries are aesthetic and civic rather than financial. And it favors the shadow docket as a policy engine — a one-paragraph, unsigned order now overrides two lower courts that actually examined the merits. The losers are congressional control over what gets built on federal land, and the plaintiffs who went to court to defend it.

## The aftermath

Construction resumes now; the litigation grinds on in the D.C. Circuit and, eventually, perhaps back here on the merits. But a stay in a construction case is close to a final word: you cannot un-demolish an East Wing or un-pour a foundation, and by the time a merits ruling arrives the ballroom may be hosting events. Expect the standing holding to be cited far beyond this case, in every future suit where the Government would rather argue that the plaintiff cannot sue than defend what it is doing. And expect Roberts's dissent — unusually pointed for him — to be read as a signal about how far even the Chief thinks this Court has drifted on the emergency docket.

## My take

Strip away the ballroom and the marquee plaintiff and what is left is a genuinely troubling move: the Court used a threshold standing argument to let the Government finish something a statute appears to forbid, before any court has finally said whether it is allowed. Standing is supposed to keep courts in their lane, not serve as a trapdoor the Government can spring whenever it wants a project to become a fait accompli. Roberts is right that the statute is the statute and Congress did not say yes. That the majority could not bring itself to engage that question — and folded the equities into national-security boilerplate — tells you the reasoning was working backward from the result. Sometimes a demolished East Wing is just a demolished East Wing. Sometimes it is a preview of how this Court plans to handle the next two years.
