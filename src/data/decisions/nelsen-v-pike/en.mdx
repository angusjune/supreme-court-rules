## What the ruling does

Hours before Tennessee planned to carry out its first execution of a woman in more than two centuries, a three-judge panel of the Sixth Circuit stopped it — not to decide that Christa Pike should live, but to buy itself time to answer a narrow procedural question before it became moot forever. The Supreme Court erased that pause the same evening. In a one-line order, with no reasoning and no signature, a 6-3 majority vacated the stay and let the State proceed. Justices Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson dissented. Nothing in the order engaged the question the Sixth Circuit had reserved; it simply took the pause away.

## The Rule 60(b) question

What the Sixth Circuit wanted to resolve was not whether Pike is guilty — she is, of the 1995 torture-killing of her nineteen-year-old Job Corps classmate Colleen Slemmer — but whether a motion she filed late in the day could even be heard. For decades the State had told courts that Pike's account of being sexually abused as a child was a lie she invented to escape a death sentence. Then, at an August hearing, it conceded the abuse was real. Pike's lawyers moved under Rule 60(b) to reopen her case on the ground that the State had reversed the very position that helped send her to death row. The Sixth Circuit stayed the execution for one reason: to decide whether that motion was a legitimate request to reopen or a disguised "second or successive" habeas petition it had no power to entertain. The Supreme Court's order means that question will now be answered, if at all, about a woman the State was free to kill first.

## What this favors

It favors finality over the orderly work of the court below. The stay here was modest and self-limiting — an appellate court protecting its own ability to decide a live issue — and the majority dissolved it anyway, without a word about why the Sixth Circuit was wrong to want the time. That is a thumb on the scale for the State in the one setting where a mistake cannot be corrected later. It also favors a posture the Court has grown comfortable with on the emergency docket: reaching down to clear an execution while saying nothing, leaving the public, the prisoner, and the lower court to guess at the reasoning behind an irreversible result.

## The aftermath

Then the execution failed. Tennessee tried for roughly an hour, pushed two doses of pentobarbital, and could not complete it; Pike survived, and witnesses described a scene gone badly wrong. Governor Bill Lee ordered a comprehensive third-party review and said no further execution would take place in the state this year. So the stay the Supreme Court was unwilling to leave in place for a few more days to let a court finish its work was followed by a botched execution and a self-imposed moratorium — the delay the justices refused to tolerate, imposed instead by the State's own machinery after the fact. The Rule 60(b) question the Sixth Circuit tried to preserve is, for now, still open, and Pike is still alive to press it.

## My take

Taking an execution off hold is the gravest thing the Court does on the shadow docket, and it is exactly the thing it should never do in silence. The Sixth Circuit asked for a little time to decide a real question it had the authority to decide; the majority gave it none and gave the rest of us no reason. What happened next is the argument the dissent could not have scripted: the State that insisted it must proceed that night could not, in the end, proceed at all. A court that will not pause for a lower court's live question, and will not explain itself when a life is on the line, has mistaken speed for rigor. Sotomayor, Kagan, and Jackson were right that some decisions deserve to wait for an answer. This one got a terrible answer anyway.
