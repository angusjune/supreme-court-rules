## What the ruling does

A centrist third party wanted the Supreme Court to force New Mexico to print two of its statewide candidates on the November ballot. Justice Neil Gorsuch, wearing his Circuit Justice hat for the Tenth Circuit, said no — in a single line, with no reasoning, and without bothering the other eight. That is the whole "decision": an emergency application denied. Bob Perls, the Forward Party chair angling for a U.S. Senate seat, and Michael Vigil, its candidate for State Auditor, are off the ballot. The lower courts had refused to intervene, and the nation's highest court just declined to second-guess them on the eve of an election.

## The silence of a one-Justice order

Do not mistake this for a ruling on whether New Mexico's law is fair. A summary denial of an emergency application decides nothing on the merits and binds no future court. The party's real grievance — that New Mexico demands roughly 14,000 signatures from a minor-party statewide hopeful while major-party candidates clear the bar with a small fraction of that, on top of a separate party-qualification petition — was never actually adjudicated here. Gorsuch's order is a timing decision dressed as nothing at all: too close to the election, too thin a record, not the emergency docket's job. The constitutional question survives; it just won't get answered before the ballots print.

## What this favors

The immediate winner is the status quo — New Mexico's Secretary of State and the two-party structure its ballot rules protect. Incumbent-friendly ballot-access thresholds are notoriously hard to dislodge, and the Court's reluctance to touch them on an emergency posture is itself a thumb on the scale for the parties already in power. It also favors the Justices' growing instinct to keep the emergency docket out of last-minute election disputes — the so-called *Purcell* reflex, that courts should not reorder an election that is already underway. Third parties, and the voters who might have preferred a third option, get nothing.

## The aftermath

Perls and Vigil sit out 2026, and Democratic incumbents Ben Ray Luján and Joseph Maestas run without a Forward Party name beside them on the statewide lines. But the underlying lawsuit is not over: the district court still has the merits of the party's equal-treatment and First Amendment challenge in front of it, unhurried by the election calendar. If the Forward Party wants a real answer, the path is an ordinary appeal on a full record, not a fire drill days before the vote. Expect this exact fight — high minor-party signature walls, tested against *Anderson–Burdick* — to come back on a slower track.

## My take

This is not the Court blessing New Mexico's ballot maze; it is the Court shrugging at the clock. And on the clock, the shrug is defensible — you cannot litigate a serious First Amendment question responsibly in the ten days before an election. But let's be honest about the cost of that defensible answer: a ballot-access regime that plausibly discriminates against everyone who isn't a Democrat or a Republican gets one more election of immunity simply because the challenge arrived late. Gorsuch gave the tidy procedural response. The messy substantive question — why a third party in New Mexico needs seven times the signatures of the incumbents — is still sitting there, unanswered, waiting for a court with the calendar to face it.
