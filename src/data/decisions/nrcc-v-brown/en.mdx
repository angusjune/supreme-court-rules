## What the ruling does

Two months before the midterms, the Supreme Court just made it cheaper for the two Republican campaign committees to flood the airwaves. In an unsigned, 8-1 order on the emergency docket, the justices stayed a Fourth Circuit ruling that had struck down the FCC's March guidance and restored the policy letting party committees buy broadcast time at the "lowest unit charge" — the rock-bottom rate that federal law guarantees candidates in the 60 days before an election. The Court did not say the parties are actually entitled to that discount. It said, in effect, not yet, not like this: the challenge was premature, the election is upon us, and the discount stays on while the appeal grinds forward. As a practical matter, that resolves the only question that mattered this cycle.

## The prematurity dodge

The majority's move here is worth naming for what it is. Rather than defend the proposition that party committees are candidates for ratemaking purposes — a genuinely contestable reading of the Communications Act — the Court reached for a threshold, procedural rationale: the challengers sued too soon, the lower court should not have set the guidance aside. It is the same maneuver the Court used in June to pause the California election-order injunction and in August on the ballroom: decide the timing, control the outcome, and leave the merits for a later day that may never functionally arrive. Justice Jackson, writing alone, called it out — the appeals panel had the authority to decide the case it decided, and there was no good reason to yank the rug out weeks before ballots and ad buys go final.

## What this favors

This favors Republicans, plainly and immediately: the NRCC and NRSC can now stretch every dollar across far more airtime than their Democratic counterparts could if the Fourth Circuit's ruling had held. It favors the administration, which backed the committees. And it favors a particular vision of campaign finance — the one the Court has been building all year, from striking coordinated-spending limits in June to this — in which the party committees operate less like independent outside groups and more like extensions of their candidates, with all the legal privileges that status carries. Broadcasters and the Democratic challengers absorb the loss.

## The aftermath

The discounted rate is live for the whole fall campaign, which is the ballgame — by the time the Fourth Circuit or this Court reaches the merits, the ads will have run and the election will be over. Expect Democratic committees to demand the same treatment rather than fight it, normalizing the very reading they challenged. And expect the "premature" label to keep doing quiet, heavy work on the emergency docket: a one-paragraph order, no full briefing, no signed opinion, and a policy with real electoral consequences left standing through the only election that counts this year.

## My take

The honest question — are national party committees entitled to the candidate discount? — is hard, and reasonable judges could land either way. What is not honest is dressing up a decisive intervention as mere restraint. Staying a lower court and reviving a policy just in time for the committees to spend on it is not staying your hand; it is choosing the winner and calling it patience. Jackson's lonely dissent has the better of it: if the challenge was really premature, the tidy answer was to let the Fourth Circuit's judgment stand and take the case in the ordinary course. Instead the Court did the opposite, fast, and unsigned. Two months out, that is not neutrality. That is a thumb on the scale, delivered on schedule.
