## What the ruling does

Ten weeks before the midterms, the Supreme Court handed the Republican campaign committees a real-money win — and did it on the emergency docket, unsigned, without deciding who is actually right. By an 8-1 vote the Court recalled and stayed the Fourth Circuit's mandate in *Brown v. FCC*, snapping the FCC's guidance back into force. That guidance lets political parties and certain joint fundraising committees buy broadcast time at the "lowest unit charge" — the steep discount Congress guarantees candidates in the 60 days before a general election. The majority pointedly did not hold that the FCC is right about who qualifies. It held that the challengers — four Democratic candidates — likely sued in the wrong place at the wrong time, because their administrative appeal was still sitting at the FCC when they ran to court. Justice Jackson, alone, would have left the appeals court's ruling standing.

## The timing is the ruling

Read the calendar and the holding blurs into one thing. The stay landed on September 4 — the first day of the lowest-unit-charge window for November. A jurisdictional pause issued on any other date is a lawyer's footnote; issued on *this* date it is a purchasing decision for every party committee in the country. The Court reached the merits of nothing and settled the only question that mattered this cycle: whose ads run cheap this fall. That is a procedural order carrying a very substantive payload.

## What this favors

This favors party committees and the coordinated-spending apparatus built around them, and it favors the FCC's expansive reading of a candidate-only discount — at least operationally, for now. It favors Republicans in particular, because the NRCC and NRSC are the applicants and the discount now flows to their fall advertising. And, as with so much of the current docket, it favors the emergency track itself as an instrument of policy: an unsigned order, no argument, no merits, reshaping the price of political speech nationwide. The losers are the candidates who actually won below and now watch that victory shelved for the duration of the only election it was ever going to touch.

## The aftermath

The discount is live for the midterms; the Fourth Circuit's merits ruling is frozen; a certiorari petition is coming, and the case may well return with a full opinion — after the ads have aired and the votes are counted. Broadcasters must re-price party buys on short notice. Expect both sides to litigate the exhaustion question hard, because the Court's jurisdictional theory, if it hardens, would quietly reroute a whole class of FCC challenges back through the agency first. Jackson's dissent is the marker to watch: if four candidates with a live grievance can be told they came too early, the "come back later" door swings in only one direction here — after the election is over.

## My take

This is a procedural stay with a partisan payload, and everyone in the building knows it. The Court avoided saying whether the FCC's reading of the statute is lawful, which is the honest, hard question, and instead leaned on a jurisdictional theory that conveniently produces a result — cheaper party ads for the fall — without owning the reasoning that would justify it. Maybe the FCC is right on the merits; maybe it isn't. But rewriting the economics of political broadcasting on the opening day of the discount window, through an unsigned order that decides nothing it claims to, is the emergency docket doing exactly what it should not: picking the winner and skipping the argument.
