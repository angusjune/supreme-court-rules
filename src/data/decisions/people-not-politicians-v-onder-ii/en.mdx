## What the ruling does

For the third time in three weeks, the Supreme Court refused to let Missouri run the November midterms on a Republican gerrymander — and this time it reversed a federal appeals court to do it. In a five-page, unsigned order the Justices stayed the Eighth Circuit's September 21 ruling, which had ordered the district court to force Secretary of State Denny Hoskins to use HB 1, the 2025 map that carves up Rep. Emanuel Cleaver's Kansas City seat and turns a 6–2 Republican delegation into a 7–1 one. The Court started where the case begins — with state law — and said it plainly: the Missouri Supreme Court has held that HB 1 never took effect, so "the 2022 map — not the 2025 map — must be used in the 2026 congressional election." No Justice publicly dissented. Ballots this fall will carry the old lines.

## The Purcell boomerang

The striking thing about this order is what it does with Purcell v. Gonzalez — the doctrine that federal courts should not rewrite election rules at the last minute. The Eighth Circuit had wielded Purcell to compel the *new* map, reasoning that because Missouri had used the 2025 lines in its August primary, switching back now would be the disruptive change. The Supreme Court turned that on its head. With voting already underway on the 2022 map, the Justices held, it is *reversion to the 2025 map* that would "usher in electoral chaos." Same doctrine, opposite result — and the Court all but told the Eighth Circuit it had read the eleventh hour backwards. Purcell protects the status quo voters are actually casting ballots under, not whichever map a court prefers on the merits.

## What this favors

The immediate winners are the petition's signatories and the organizers of People Not Politicians, whose referendum survives and whose objection to HB 1 will be settled by voters rather than by a mapmaker. Democrats gain too: Cleaver's district lives another cycle and the GOP's coveted seventh seat is off the board for 2026. But the deeper winner is the same boundary the Court guarded two weeks ago — the primacy of a state's own high court on questions of its own law. The referendum side lost at the Eighth Circuit and won here precisely because the Justices refused to let a federal court override Missouri's reading of the Missouri Constitution weeks before an election. That is a federalism holding dressed as an emergency stay.

## The aftermath

Missouri votes in November on the 2022 districts, and Proposition A stays on the ballot. But this is still the emergency docket, not a merits judgment: the federal suit brought by Rep. Bob Onder and candidate Rick Brattin returns to the lower courts, and nothing here forecloses a ruling that reopens the map for a later cycle. Expect the losing side to press its constitutional theory on remand, and expect other states mid-redistricting — Texas, Ohio, the rest of the mid-decade scramble — to read this order as a warning about how far a federal court can go to swap a map on the eve of voting. The one thing now settled is the thing that matters most in September: the clerks have a single map to print.

## My take

Three trips to the Supreme Court, three losses for the Missouri gerrymander, and the reasoning has only gotten cleaner. The first two orders were near-wordless shrugs; this one is five pages that finally say the quiet part — a state's highest court, not a federal panel, reads the state's constitution, and Purcell shields the ballots voters are already marking, not the map a court would rather see. I would have liked a signature on it, because a principle stated this clearly deserves an author. But on the merits this is the emergency docket doing its job: freezing a last-second federal injunction so an election can proceed on settled ground. The map fight will grind on. The 2026 election will not wait for it.
