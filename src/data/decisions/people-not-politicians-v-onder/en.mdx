## What the ruling does

For the second time in three days, the Supreme Court kept Missouri from running the November midterms on a Republican gerrymander — and it did so by *stopping* a federal court, not a state one. In a one-page, unsigned order the Justices stayed the temporary restraining order that Chief U.S. District Judge Stephen R. Clark had entered, which would have forced Secretary of State Denny Hoskins to conduct the election under HB 1, the map the legislature drew in 2025 to carve up Rep. Emanuel Cleaver's Kansas City seat and turn a 6–2 Republican delegation into a 7–1 one. With the TRO frozen, the Missouri Supreme Court's September 3 decision snaps back into force: HB 1 never legally took effect, the state votes in November on the 2022 lines, and the referendum on the map goes to the people as Proposition A. The Court disclosed no vote and noted no dissent, and it decided none of the hard questions the case teed up.

## The three-map week

To see why this order matters, follow the week it capped. On September 3 the Missouri Supreme Court unanimously held that a 300,000-signature referendum petition had suspended HB 1, so the old map governed. On September 8 Justice Kavanaugh, the Circuit Justice for the Eighth Circuit, declined to disturb that ruling — and minutes later Judge Clark, ruling in a federal suit brought by Rep. Bob Onder, GOP candidate Rick Brattin, and two voters, ordered the state to use HB 1 anyway. For two days Missouri had two courts pointing at two different maps and a filing deadline bearing down. Thursday's stay is the tie-break: the federal injunction yields, the state judgment stands, and election clerks finally have one set of lines to print.

## What this favors

The immediate winners are the voters who signed the petition and the organizers who ran it — the referendum survives, and the map they object to will not be imposed before they get to vote on it. Democrats benefit too: Cleaver's district lives another cycle and the GOP's hoped-for seventh seat is off the board for 2026. But the deeper thing this favors is a boundary the Court has lately been quick to erase — the line between federal courts and a state's own reading of its own constitution. The applicants' pitch was structural: a single district judge should not, weeks before an election, override a state supreme court on a question of state law, least of all without the three-judge court that federal law demands for congressional-districting fights. The stay vindicates that boundary in result, even as the unsigned order declines to say so in words.

## The aftermath

Missouri will run November on the 2022 map, and Proposition A stays on the ballot; the federal case, though, is only paused, not resolved. Onder and Brattin's suit returns to the district court and likely the Eighth Circuit, and nothing here forecloses a merits ruling that could reopen the map question for a later cycle. Expect the standing and three-judge-court arguments the applicants pressed to become the center of gravity on remand — and expect other states mid-redistricting to read this order as a signal about how far a lone federal judge can go on the eve of an election. The contempt cloud over Hoskins in the state courts has not lifted either. What is settled is narrow but real: the ballots that voters mark this fall will carry the old districts.

## My take

The Court got the emergency right, and got it right the quiet way. A federal TRO that commandeers a state election on a disputed reading of state law, issued without the three-judge court Congress required, is exactly the kind of order that should not survive contact with a stay — and it didn't. I wish the Justices had signed their name to that reasoning instead of leaving a one-paragraph order to do the work, because the principle here is worth stating out loud: federal courts do not get to redraw a state's ballot at the last minute when the state's own highest court has already spoken. But on the merits of the moment, this is the emergency docket used for what it is actually for — freezing a chaotic, last-second injunction so an election can proceed on settled ground.
