## What the ruling does

Eight weeks before the midterms, the Supreme Court pulled Missouri back from the edge of an election run under a map that its own highest court had declared void. In an unsigned order with no noted dissents, the Justices stayed a federal district judge's eleventh-hour command that Missouri use HB 1 — the Trump-backed redraw engineered to hand Republicans seven of the state's eight House seats. Freeze that federal order, and the Missouri Supreme Court's September 3 judgment snaps back into force: HB 1 "is not the law and has never been the law." Voters on November 3 will use the districts Missouri has run since 2022. The Court decided nothing about who is ultimately right; it decided who governs the ballot that is already being printed, and it chose the status quo over a last-minute gerrymander.

## The two-court collision

This case is a lesson in how not to run an election. Missouri's Supreme Court, interpreting state law, said HB 1 never took effect and belongs on the ballot as Proposition A. Days later a federal district judge in the Eastern District of Missouri issued a temporary restraining order commanding state officials to use HB 1 and nothing else — the mirror image of the state court's injunction. Secretaries of state cannot obey two courts pointing in opposite directions, and county clerks cannot print two sets of districts. The Eighth Circuit declined to intervene. By staying the federal order, the Justices did the one thing that restored a single, administrable answer before ballots locked — and they did it without a single public dissent, which on today's Court is its own kind of signal.

## What this favors

This favors the electoral status quo, the finality of state courts on questions of state law, and the principle that federal judges should not redraw the districts of a pending election by emergency fiat. It favors People Not Politicians and the more than 300,000 Missourians who signed to put the map to a popular vote. Practically, it favors Democrats, who keep a competitive seat that HB 1 was drawn to erase, and it denies the White House the extra Missouri seat its mid-decade redistricting push was chasing. And it favors calm over chaos: whatever one thinks of the merits, an election is not the place to run a live experiment in dueling injunctions.

## The aftermath

The 2022 map is locked for November; Proposition A puts HB 1's fate to the voters; the merits move to the Eighth Circuit with a certiorari petition waiting in the wings. The gerrymander is paused, not buried — if the federal plaintiffs ultimately prevail, this fight returns, likely with a full opinion and after the votes are counted. Expect the mid-decade redistricting wars in other states to absorb the lesson that the Court, at least this once, would not bless a map its own state supreme court had killed. Watch, too, for how hard the Justices lean on the "too late to change the rules" logic when the next state tries the same maneuver from the opposite partisan direction.

## My take

An emergency order that produces the least disruptive result, commands every Justice, and refuses to pretend it has settled the merits is the emergency docket working the way it should — a rarity worth naming out loud. The Court did not endorse Missouri's voters or rebuke Missouri's legislature; it simply refused to let a federal judge conscript an already-running election into a partisan map the state's own courts had voided. Right result, right restraint, and a unanimous one at that. The real contest over HB 1 now belongs to the people who will vote on Proposition A — which is exactly where a fight over the shape of Missouri's democracy ought to be decided.
