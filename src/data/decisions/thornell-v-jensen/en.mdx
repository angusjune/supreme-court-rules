## What the ruling does

A single Justice, on a single line, let one of the most dramatic remedies in American prison litigation go forward. Arizona had spent fourteen years fighting the claim that its prison health care was unconstitutional, and after it burned through warnings, contempt fines, and a broken settlement, a federal judge took the keys away and handed them to an outside receiver. The State ran to the Supreme Court for a last-minute pause. Justice Elena Kagan, sitting as Circuit Justice for the Ninth Circuit, said no. The order carried no reasoning and was not sent to the full Court — but its effect was total: the receiver takes over health care for about 25,000 people on October 19.

## Fourteen years of noncompliance

A receivership is not where prison cases usually start; it is where they end when nothing else has worked. Arizona agreed in 2014 to fix medical and mental-health care behind its walls, then spent years failing to, drawing more than $2 million in contempt sanctions. Judge Roslyn O. Silver eventually revoked the settlement, found the State had shown little genuine interest in change, and concluded that only an outside manager with real authority could bring the system into constitutional compliance. By the time the application reached Kagan, three layers of federal courts had already looked at the record and declined to intervene. The "emergency" was one of Arizona's own making.

## What this favors

It favors the incarcerated plaintiffs and the ordinary machinery of federal equity. The receivership is a structural remedy — a court seizing operational control because repeated, documented constitutional violations went unfixed — and the denial lets it function on the district court's timeline rather than the State's. It also favors judicial deference running downward: a Circuit Justice declining to second-guess a trial judge's hard-won factual findings and a court of appeals that saw no reason to stay them. For once, the emergency docket produced a quiet result that simply let the courts below do their jobs.

## The aftermath

On October 19, Annette Chambers-Smith — who ran Ohio's corrections department — assumes control of health care for Arizona's prison population, with the independence a receiver needs to hire, spend, and override the department. Arizona's appeals of the receivership and of her appointment continue, but the handover is no longer paused, and unwinding a functioning receivership is far harder than blocking one before it starts. Expect fights over the receiver's budget and authority, and over how long the arrangement lasts — receiverships are meant to be temporary, yet they end only when the State proves it can run a constitutional system on its own.

## My take

This is what the emergency docket should look like: no drama, no unsigned rewrite of the law, just a Justice refusing to rescue a State from the consequences of its own fourteen-year record. Arizona wanted the Supreme Court to treat "we would like more time" as an emergency. It is not. A court does not reach for a receiver until every lesser tool has failed, and Kagan rightly declined to pretend otherwise. The real test now is whether Arizona finally builds a prison health system that can function without a court-appointed manager — because that, and only that, is the exit.
