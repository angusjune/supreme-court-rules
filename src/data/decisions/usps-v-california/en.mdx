## What the ruling does

With ballots already going out for the midterms, the Supreme Court left in place the block on the Trump administration's new mail-voting regime. The order is unsigned and narrow — it decides only that the Government is unlikely to win and that the equities do not justify emergency relief weeks before an election — but its practical effect is enormous. The Postal Service's Final Rule, which would have forced states to redesign ballot envelopes with a scannable barcode tied to each voter and to feed voter lists into a USPS portal before ballots could ship, cannot be used in 2026. For once the emergency docket produced a result that protects the mechanics of voting rather than disrupting them.

## Purcell, turned around

The fascinating thing here is the machinery of Kavanaugh's concurrence. The "too close to the election" principle — that courts should not change the rules as voters are already casting ballots — is usually deployed to *stop judges* from touching a state's election system. Kavanaugh took that same logic and pointed it at the executive branch: it is the administration's own last-minute rule, dropped fewer than seventy days before November, that arrives too late to be imposed without chaos. He is careful to say the rule may well be lawful in the abstract. But a rule that election officials cannot possibly implement in time is, he concludes, arbitrary and capricious under the Administrative Procedure Act. The Purcell reflex, for once, cut against the party trying to rewrite the rules on the eve of an election.

## What this favors

This favors voters who rely on mail ballots, the states that run elections, and the ordinary expectation that the ground rules are settled before the season begins. It also favors a modest reading of the emergency docket: the Court declined to hand the administration a sweeping win on a thin record and a compressed clock. The coalition behind the win is worth noting — a lopsided Court, with only Alito and Thomas in dissent and Kavanaugh writing to explain why even a possibly-valid rule cannot be sprung on the system this late. When seven Justices, across the ideological spectrum, decline to disturb an injunction, the signal to the administration is unambiguous.

## The aftermath

The injunction holds through the 2026 midterms, so no state has to redesign envelopes, buy new scanners, or upload voter rolls to a federal portal this cycle. The litigation over whether the Postal Service actually has the statutory authority to impose these requirements continues in the lower courts, and the Court pointedly did not resolve it — Alito's dissent is a roadmap for the administration to try again on a fuller record and a longer runway. Expect the fight to return, likely aimed at 2028 rather than the ballots already in the mail. For now, the envelopes that voters receive will look the way they always have.

## My take

This is the emergency docket working the way it is supposed to: cautiously, and with a bias against upending a live election on short notice. The administration wanted the Court to bless a brand-new voting rule that even a sympathetic Justice concedes landed too late to run. The Court said no, and the two dissenters could not muster a third vote. Whatever one thinks of barcoded envelopes as policy, the timing was indefensible, and Kavanaugh's opinion says so in as many words. Change the rules of an election, if you must, before the game has started — not while the ballots are in the mail.
